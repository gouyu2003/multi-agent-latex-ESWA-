2.2.2. Rationale for the transition from single-agent to multi-agent systems
     Single-agent architectures remain a strong baseline for tasks that can be handled by one workflow, but their limitations
become apparent when a problem requires cross-domain knowledge, long-horizon reasoning, extensive evidence, or high
reliability. A single model must understand the query, retrieve and assess evidence, generate an answer, and possibly verify its
own output. This concentration of functions creates three related risks: incomplete knowledge and hallucination in unfamiliar
domains, dilution of relevant evidence in long contexts, and the absence of an independent perspective in a one-pass answer.
Self-refinement and tool use can mitigate these risks, but they do not remove the underlying dependence on one model and
one reasoning trajectory.
     The motivation for multi-agent systems is therefore capability extension rather than simple replacement. Inspired by
Minsky’s Society of Mind (Minsky, 1986), multi-agent architectures can combine agents with individual memory, reflection,
and planning, as illustrated in social simulations (Park et al., 2023). A single “generalist” workflow may combine query
understanding, retrieval, generation, and refinement, whereas a multi-agent workflow can separate these functions into, for
example, Planner, Retriever, Generator, and Critic roles. MetaGPT demonstrates role allocation among product managers,
architects, and engineers in software development (Hong et al., 2024), while CAMEL studies sustained interaction between
user and assistant roles (Li et al., 2023). These examples show how role conditioning can structure collaboration, but they do
not establish a universal advantage: specialization is useful only when roles have genuinely different information, tools, or
verification responsibilities.
     This division of labour can extend both knowledge coverage and effective context capacity. Different agents may use
complementary prompts, tools, or evidence sources, so diversity is beneficial when it contributes independent evidence rather
than duplicated model calls. Likewise, segmenting a large document across agents can support broader coverage than a single
context, as explored by LongAgent and Chain-of-Agents (Zhao et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). These gains are conditional:
intermediate results must still be communicated and synthesized, and aggregation loss, communication cost, and the final
synthesis context can become new bottlenecks.
     Multi-agent collaboration can also improve reasoning reliability by creating separate candidate, review, and verification
paths. Debate, cross-checking, and reconciliation may expose errors that self-refinement within one workflow overlooks (Du
et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024a; Xiong et al., 2023). However, additional agents do not guarantee correctness: weak aggregation,
poor interaction design, or correlated errors can preserve or amplify an incorrect answer. Coordination quality is thus a central
design variable, not an incidental implementation detail.


3. Architectures of multi-agent collaborative QA systems
3.1. Taxonomy of multi-agent collaborative QA architectures
    This review classifies a collaborative QA system primarily by the distribution of decision authority and control. The
classification is based on the workflow that routes subtasks, determines when an answer is accepted, and controls final synthesis.
A flat architecture has no persistent agent with top-down authority over the other participants. A hierarchical architecture
assigns planning, routing, approval, or synthesis authority to one or more managers, or imposes an ordered, asymmetric
control flow across stages. A hybrid architecture changes this authority structure across task stages or decision states. Other
design considerations such as communication topology (for example, broadcast, peer exchange, or a graph), role specialization,
human-in-the-loop (HITL) checkpoints, and runtime routing are orthogonal attributes. For example, a group-chat framework
is not automatically flat, and a system with specialized roles is not automatically hierarchical. The three classes are alternative
control patterns rather than stages of a historical progression. Figure 3 provides an illustrative schematic.

3.1.1. Flat architectures
    In a flat collaborative architecture, participating agents exchange information, critique candidate solutions, or engage in
negotiations without a persistent top-down controller. The relevant characteristic lies in distributed decision-making authority,
rather than unrestricted dialogue. A flat workflow may still enforce turn limits, adopt voting rules, or include a designated final
aggregation step.

Multi-agent debate. In the multi-agent debate paradigm, several LLM agents generate candidate answers and critique
one another across one or more rounds. Representative studies illustrate both the potential and the limits of this design. For
example, Du et al. (2024) report improved factuality and reasoning performance on the tasks they evaluate, whereas divergent
prompting can mitigate convergence toward similar reasoning paths (Liang et al., 2024). ReConcile provides another example
by integrating heterogeneous models with confidence-weighted consensus (Chen et al., 2024a); inter-model inconsistency and
capability imbalance nevertheless remain important considerations (Xiong et al., 2023). Accordingly, adding more agents or
debate rounds does not reliably boost QA performance; instead, the interaction rules, diversity of evidence, and aggregation
procedure determine whether disagreement yields correction, inconsistency, or unnecessary computational cost.

Role-conditioned collaboration. Role-conditioned collaboration assigns different prompts or task views to participating
agents. For example, CAMEL (Li et al., 2023) employs an "AI User" and an "AI Assistant" paired with inception prompting
to maintain role-conditioned interaction. This constitutes an adjacent multi-agent framework and does not serve as direct
evidence that role playing improves QA accuracy.

Group-chat collaboration. Group-chat collaboration adopts multi-participant conversations as its interaction interface. For
example, AutoGen (Wu et al., 2024) supports developer-defined speaker selection rules and conversation patterns instead
of enforcing a fully flat, free-form group chat paradigm. Accordingly, its resulting topology is determined by application
configurations.
    Flat architectures can be useful when independent viewpoints, mutual critique, or open-ended exploration are required.
Their distributed structure can reduce reliance on a single reasoning path, yet it also incurs coordination costs. In fully
connected settings, the number of potential communication links grows quadratically with the number of agents. Additional
rounds may introduce redundant context, delayed convergence, correlated errors, or pseudo-consensus. By contrast, when
tasks demand stable dependencies, predictable execution, or stringent cost control, explicit authority and routing mechanisms
may be more appropriate.

3.1.2. Hierarchical architectures
    Hierarchical architectures delegate the authority for planning, routing, approval, or synthesis to one or more agents.
Lower-level agents execute bounded subtasks or provide evidence to a manager. The workflow may take the form of a tree, a
pipeline, or a controller–worker loop; its defining characteristic is asymmetric decision-making authority.

Manager–executor pattern. The manager–executor pattern separates global planning from local execution. For example,
MetaGPT (Hong et al., 2024) adopts standard operating procedures and defines roles including product manager, architect,
and engineer. ChatDev (Qian et al., 2024) provides another example by simulating a software development organisation via
ChatChain. Both fall under related software engineering systems; they demonstrate structured delegation yet do not provide
direct evidence regarding QA performance.

Plan–retrieve–generate pipeline. Knowledge-intensive QA can assign planning, retrieval, generation, and verification to a
staged workflow. Representative implementations include LONGAGENT (Zhao et al., 2024), which coordinates a leader with
document-reading members; Chain-of-Agents (Zhang et al., 2024), which propagates information across context segments
before synthesis; and CoCoA-zero (Jiang et al., 2026), which coordinates parametric and retrieved knowledge using three
agents at inference time. CoCoA subsequently distils that collaboration into a single model. These role-I systems differ in
their communication and aggregation mechanisms, but each places subtask sequencing or synthesis under explicit control. By
contrast, HuggingGPT (Shen et al., 2023) uses a controller to invoke specialised models and tools.
    Hierarchical architectures can improve controllability by separating global planning from local execution. They are suitable
for QA workflows with stable procedures, explicit dependencies, or quality-control checkpoints. However, this centralisation
may introduce a planner bottleneck: an erroneous high-level plan can propagate errors, and rigid pipelines may fail to handle
unexpected evidence or conflicting expert opinions. Therefore, the associated benefits depend on the quality of the controller
and the availability of corrective pathways.

3.1.3. Hybrid architectures
    Hybrid architectures alter the control configuration across task stages or decision states. A system may adopt peer
deliberation to explore candidate solutions, hierarchical delegation to execute a selected plan, and conditional human oversight
in scenarios where uncertainty or risk exceeds a predefined threshold. Simply combining individual components is insufficient;
the coordination paradigm itself must be adaptive.

Graph-structured agent networks. Graph-structured networks represent agents and transitions as an executable graph.
For example, LangGraph (LangChain Team, 2024) serves as an official technical framework for defining state transitions,
loops, and conditional branches; it provides technical evidence (role T), rather than constituting a QA evaluation. GPTSwarm
(Zhuge et al., 2024) provides another example through an empirical study of agent methodologies that optimises prompts and
inter-agent graph connections.

Adaptive collaboration-mode switching. Adaptive systems adjust team composition or control patterns as tasks evolve.
For example, AgentVerse (Chen et al., 2024b) describes an iterative pipeline consisting of expert recruitment, collaborative
decision-making, action execution, and result evaluation. This mechanism cannot be simplified to a "simple-flat/complex-
hierarchical" rule. MDAgents (Kim et al., 2024b) provides direct in-case evidence for adaptive medical collaboration. Such
adaptive mechanisms enhance routing flexibility, yet introduce extra requirements for state management and performance
evaluation.

Human-in-the-loop design pattern. For example, LangGraph supports checkpointed state and pausing a workflow with an
interrupt for human review (The LangChain Team, 2024), which may be useful for high-risk decisions.
    In a collaborative QA workflow, human participation can be placed at several points: before task decomposition, after
retrieval, when agents disagree, or before an answer is released or an external action is executed. These points serve different
purposes. Early review can correct the interpretation of the question and restrict the permitted tools; intermediate review can
assess the quality and provenance of retrieved evidence; and final review can provide an approval boundary for consequential
recommendations. The appropriate point is therefore determined by the potential impact of an error and by the extent to which
the relevant state remains understandable and editable.
    The reviewer should also have an explicitly defined level of authority. A human may merely approve a proposed answer,
select among agent outputs, edit evidence or intermediate conclusions, or redirect the workflow by requesting another retrieval
or specialist analysis. These forms of participation are not equivalent: approval without access to the supporting evidence
provides limited control, whereas unrestricted editing can make the origin of the final answer difficult to reconstruct. A useful
HITL design should preserve the question, retrieved sources, agent contributions, reviewer intervention, and resulting state
transition in an auditable record.
    HITL checkpoints can improve accountability and provide a recovery path when agents encounter uncertainty, conflicting
evidence, or unsafe requests. They also introduce review latency, operator workload, and the possibility of inconsistent
decisions across reviewers. For this reason, a fixed human breakpoint is not automatically preferable to automated execution.
Risk- adaptive policies that trigger review using uncertainty, disagreement, evidence quality, or action impact are a plausible
design, but their effects on error interception, workload, and answer quality require empirical evaluation. The current literature
therefore supports HITL as a control pattern and design objective, rather than as established evidence of safer QA performance.

    Hybrid architectures are useful when task requirements are heterogeneous or when the workflow must combine deliberation,
controlled execution, and conditional review. Their trade-off lies in engineering complexity: dynamic routing, state transitions,
and cross-mode information transfer are more difficult to configure, debug, evaluate, and reproduce. A hybrid design is justified
when this additional adaptability addresses documented uncertainty or risk, rather than serving as a default replacement for
fixed control patterns.

3.2. Architecture selection approach
    The taxonomy describes alternative control patterns; it does not imply that multi-agent collaboration is preferable for every
QA task. Architecture selection must also account for the task, process, resource, and governance conditions under which
collaboration is justified. The approach below treats a single-agent workflow as a valid baseline and produces a reasoned
shortlist of candidate architectures rather than a universal assignment.

3.2.1. Scope and selection criteria
    Four criteria connect task requirements to the control patterns in the taxonomy. They support transparent comparison
across studies, but do not define universal numerical thresholds or claim that one architecture dominates the others.
    1. Task complexity and domain span. The analysis starts from the reasoning structure, evidence requirements, output
criteria, and domain span. The criterion captures the number and interdependence of reasoning steps, the breadth of required
expertise, and the need for independent evidence sources. Single-agent execution is often adequate for short, low-uncertainty
queries; decomposition and role specialization become more plausible when a task combines separable subtasks or domains.
Complexity alone, however, does not establish that additional agents will improve the answer: each role must contribute
distinct information or checks.
    2. Process standardization and dynamism. Stable stages and explicit dependencies favor hierarchical or pipeline control,
whereas open-ended tasks may benefit from peer generation and critique. When the appropriate mode changes across queries
or stages, hybrid routing is a candidate design. These are tendencies rather than deterministic mappings: hierarchical workflows
can contain local debate, and flat workflows can enforce turn limits and aggregation rules.

    3. Cost and resource burden. Multi-agent execution adds communication, aggregation, state-management, and inference

useful benefits. As a design criterion, this review requires expected gains in coverage, reliability, or adaptability to justify the

evidence must be retained. Healthcare, legal, financial, and other consequential applications require provenance, review,
contestability, and explicit responsibility. HITL checkpoints are possible controls, but their placement and scope depend on
the decision and applicable governance requirements and must be evaluated rather than assumed.

3.2.2. Two-phase architecture-selection procedure
    The four criteria are combined in the two-phase flow shown in Fig. 4. The flow is a screening heuristic: it narrows the
plausible design space and makes trade-offs explicit, but does not replace empirical comparison of candidate implementations.
    The procedure is developed as a design artifact following the Design Science Research Methodology (DSRM) (Peffers et al.,
2007). Problem identification and objective definition motivate a transparent selection procedure; design and development
translate the architecture and technology review into the four criteria; demonstration uses contrasting QA scenarios; and
verification compares the framework’s interpretations with published systems. The result is a structured design proposal whose
assumptions remain open to empirical testing.
    Phase 1 (pre-screening). Establish whether one workflow can meet the required quality and cost. If so, retain a single-agent
baseline. If the task requires separable expertise, long-horizon reasoning, or independent verification, retain multi-agent
designs for the second phase. Use risk and auditability to constrain the permissible degree of autonomy.
    Phase 2 (topology selection). Compare process structure and information dependencies. Stable stages and explicit hand-offs
support hierarchical control; peer generation and critique support flat collaboration when independent perspectives are valuable;
changing requirements or conditional review support hybrid control. Compare the selected design with a single-agent baseline
using task quality, latency, cost, provenance, and failure-recovery measures.

3.2.3. Verification and illustrative applications
    The approach is verified for interpretive consistency by comparing its inferred architectures with ten published systems.
This purposive sample was selected because the systems report agent roles and control flow with enough detail for coding and
cover different domains, process structures, and risk levels. The comparison does not evaluate system performance, establish
causal benefit, or claim external validity.
    Five cases are broadly aligned, three are partially aligned, and two are divergent under the coding used here. MedAgents
and LLM-MedQA combine parallel expert perspectives with staged synthesis; MAD is flat at the macro level but uses iterative
convergence; EduPlanner and Financial Reflection QA combine convergent objectives with evaluator or critic feedback. These
boundary cases show that competing requirements can make a single categorical label insufficient and that a multi-label
description of control flow may sometimes be preferable.

3.2.4. Summary and limitations
    The approach makes architecture selection explicit through four criteria, a two-phase flowchart, illustrative cases, and a
diagnostic comparison with ten published systems. Its limitations are substantial: competing requirements can make a single
topology label inadequate; the framework and model ecosystem change rapidly; the purposive sample cannot establish external
validity; and the criteria remain qualitative rather than calibrated on a large labelled system corpus. Independent coders,
larger preregistered samples, quantitative cost and quality measures, and prospective comparisons with alternative selection
procedures are needed.


4. Key technologies in multi-agent collaborative QA
    The efficient operation of a multi-agent collaborative QA system depends not only on a well-designed organizational
architecture but also on the support of core underlying technologies. This section investigates five technological dimensions:
communication mechanisms, task decomposition, memory management, knowledge management, and reasoning mechanisms.

4.1. Agent communication mechanisms
    Agent communication mechanisms determine how information is exchanged among agents during collaborative QA. They
support the transfer of queries, intermediate findings, evidence, and feedback, and thereby shape coordination, traceability,
and communication cost. Communication may occur through direct messages or through changes to a shared environment;
interoperability protocols provide a related interface for connecting agents with tools and data sources. The following
subsections distinguish these interaction mechanisms from the protocols that support cross-system communication.

                                            Multi-Agent Collaborative QA: A Survey

4.1.1. Explicit communication
    Explicit communication refers to the direct transfer of information between agents via predefined formats. The most
common form is message passing based on structured languages (JSON, XML) or dedicated prompt templates. For example,
AutoGen (Wu et al., 2024) provides a representative implementation: customisable conversable agents send and receive
natural-language or code messages following conversation patterns defined by developers. ChatDev (Qian et al., 2024) provides
another example by organising role-specialised agents through a multi-turn dialogue chain covering design, coding, and testing
stages.

4.1.2. Implicit communication
    Implicit communication does not require direct agent-to-agent messaging; instead, collaboration is mediated via
modifications to a shared operating environment. For instance, in a code-generation QA task, one agent alters the state
of a code repository or database, while another agent obtains feedback by observing compilation errors or test execution
results.
    This paradigm can reduce redundant message transfers, though its impact on communication overhead and fault tolerance
is contingent on the implementation and task environment.

4.1.3. Standardised communication protocols
    Industry initiatives address different parts of the interface problem. Anthropic introduced the Model Context Protocol
(MCP) (Anthropic, 2024) to standardise connections to external tools and data sources. Google’s Agent2Agent (A2A) protocol
(Google, 2025) supports communication and task coordination between agents across frameworks and vendors. These interfaces
can support heterogeneous multi-agent QA systems.
    Explicit messaging makes the content and provenance of an agent’s contribution inspectable, and is useful when agents
must exchange evidence, explain decisions, or coordinate across distinct roles. Its main cost is message volume: repeated
dialogue consumes context and adds latency, particularly as the team grows. Implicit communication reduces direct message
passing by using shared environmental state, but it depends on agents being able to observe and interpret that state consistently;
otherwise, changes may be ambiguous or difficult to audit. Standardized protocols address a different problem. MCP and A2A
aim to improve interoperability between tools, data sources, and agents, but protocol compatibility alone does not guarantee
useful coordination or reliable QA. Thus, explicit and implicit communication are interaction mechanisms, whereas protocols
provide an interface layer that may support either. Selection should reflect the need for traceability, the availability of shared
state, and the expected interaction cost (Wu et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024; Anthropic, 2024; Google, 2025).

4.2. Task decomposition and scheduling
   Task decomposition and scheduling determine how a complex QA query is divided into manageable work and how that
work is assigned and coordinated. Their purpose is to make interdependent reasoning steps tractable, enable specialisation or
parallel execution where appropriate, and ensure that sub-results can be combined into a coherent answer. The discussion
proceeds from decomposing a query, to assigning subtasks to suitable agents, and then to optimising or adapting their execution.

4.2.1. Hierarchical task decomposition
    For complex QA tasks, LLMs often cannot answer in a single step. Hierarchical task decomposition reduces difficulty by
breaking complex queries into sub-tasks that can be processed in parallel or sequentially. Representative methods include
Least-to-Most Prompting (Zhou et al., 2023) and Decomposed Prompting (Khot et al., 2023), which allow a model to solve
simpler sub-problems first and then exploit their answers to resolve the ultimate target. Plan-and-Solve (Wang et al., 2023a)
provides another example by explicitly separating planning from solving and reducing missing-step errors on the evaluated
reasoning tasks. Formally, given a complex query 𝑄, a decomposition function  maps it to an ordered sequence of sub-tasks:

4.2.2. Role-based task assignment
    After decomposition, the system must assign sub-tasks to suitable agents. For example, MetaGPT (Hong et al., 2024)
organises agents through predefined software-engineering roles, while AgentVerse (Chen et al., 2024b) recruits experts by
generating task-specific expertise descriptions and adjusts team composition using evaluation feedback. These mechanisms
guide which agents contribute to a task; their scheduling efficiency requires separate evaluation.

4.2.3. Pipeline optimisation and adaptive orchestration
     Static assignment can be inefficient when task difficulty and agent capabilities vary across queries. For example, DSPy
(Khattab et al., 2024) addresses a related but narrower problem: it represents language-model pipelines as declarative modules
and compiles their prompts and demonstrations against a task metric. It should therefore be regarded as a pipeline-optimization
toolkit rather than evidence supporting runtime multi-agent load balancing. Dynamic agent recruitment, routing, and early
stopping remain distinct orchestration decisions that require task-specific evaluation.
     Decomposition, assignment, and orchestration solve related but distinct problems. Decomposition determines which
sub-questions must be answered and how they depend on one another; assignment matches those sub-questions to agents;
orchestration determines their execution order and whether the workflow should adapt at runtime. Least-to-Most, Decomposed
Prompting, and Plan-and-Solve primarily inform the first decision, whereas role profiles in MetaGPT and AgentVerse address
the second (Zhou et al., 2023; Khot et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023a; Hong et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024b). A fixed sequence is
adequate when dependencies and agent capabilities are known in advance; dynamic routing may be more suitable when query
difficulty or available expertise varies, but it adds scheduling overhead and failure points. In either case, decomposition errors
can propagate: omitted sub-questions leave gaps in the final answer, while unnecessary decomposition creates redundant calls
and aggregation work. Pipeline-optimization tools such as DSPy can improve task-metric performance of language-model
modules, but should not be treated as evidence that runtime recruitment or load balancing is effective (Khattab et al., 2024).
Evaluation should distinguish decomposition quality, assignment fit, and orchestration efficiency rather than attributing an
end-to-end gain to a single mechanism.

4.3. Knowledge management
    Knowledge management concerns how external, retrieved, and agent-generated information is represented, validated, and
made available to the team. It provides a shared evidence basis for collaborative QA and aims to reduce duplicated information
seeking without allowing unverified content to spread. It is therefore a system-level layer built on, but not equivalent to,
individual agent memory.
    In complex collaboration, knowledge must not be siloed within individual agents. The system needs a global knowledge
graph shared across agents so that all collaborative nodes can synchronise critical facts in real time. By constructing a shared
graph-vector database, newly spawned sub-agents can rapidly inherit the team’s cumulative knowledge, avoiding redundant
retrieval and cognitive divergence.
    Knowledge management operates above individual memory. A shared graph or vector store can provide a common evidence
base and reduce duplicated retrieval, but unrestricted sharing can also spread stale, irrelevant, or erroneous content across the
team. Practical designs should attach provenance, timestamps, confidence, and validation status to shared facts, and should
allow agents to revise or withdraw them. The key design questions are which knowledge is shared, which agents may access it,
how conflicts are resolved, and how retrieved or generated content is verified before it becomes reusable team knowledge.

4.4. Memory management
    Memory management concerns the information state retained by an agent or workflow over time. It preserves relevant
context across interaction turns and supports continuity when a task spans multiple reasoning steps. It is distinct from knowledge
management: memory concerns retention and recall of state, whereas knowledge management concerns the organisation,
validation, and distribution of reusable information.

4.4.1. Short-term memory and working memory
    Short-term memory preserves dialogue history and local context for the current QA session. For example, MemGPT
(Packer et al., 2023) draws on operating-system memory hierarchies to manage a limited main context and external memory.
Function calls control information transfer between them, while a FIFO message queue and recursive summaries manage
dialogue context under the context-window limit.

    Long-term memory can retain historical experiences and domain knowledge in external stores (Zhang et al., 2025b). Vector
retrieval is one way to access such information: Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) (Lewis et al., 2020; Gao et al., 2023)
augments generation with retrieved documents. As one illustrative multi-agent implementation, a retrieval agent can select the

4.5. Reasoning mechanisms
   Reasoning mechanisms aim to improve answer quality by broadening the available reasoning paths, distributing evidence
processing, or revisiting provisional conclusions to detect errors. In collaborative QA, these methods can complement one
another, but they target different constraints and may add inference cost. The following categories cover collaborative reasoning
and answer aggregation, reasoning distributed across agents, and reflection or critique of generated answers.

4.5.1. Collaborative chain-of-thought
    Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting (Wei et al., 2022) can improve performance on evaluated arithmetic, commonsense,
and symbolic reasoning tasks. Self-Consistency (Wang et al., 2023b) provides a representative single-model method: it samples
multiple reasoning paths and selects an answer by marginalising over those paths. Multi-agent systems adapt this diversity
principle by assigning independent reasoners and then using debate or confidence-aware aggregation (Du et al., 2024; Chen
et al., 2024a).

4.5.2. Chain-of-agents reasoning
    For ultra-long-text QA, the attention mechanism of conventional Transformers faces severe computational bottlenecks.
Chain-of-Agents (Zhang et al., 2024) distributes evidence processing across worker agents, while Tree of Thoughts (Yao
et al., 2023a) and Graph of Thoughts (Besta et al., 2024) organise intermediate reasoning states as search structures. In
Chain-of-Agents, worker agents process successive text chunks and pass forward condensed evidence before a manager
produces the answer. LONGAGENT (Zhao et al., 2024) uses a different leader–member design in which a leader coordinates
member agents over long-document segments. Tree- and graph-structured reasoning provide related search topologies, but are
not necessarily multi-agent systems in their original formulations.

4.5.3. Self-reflection and mutual-critique mechanisms
    To suppress hallucination, agents must be capable of iterative self-examination. For example, Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023)
stores linguistic feedback from prior trials in episodic memory, whereas Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023) provides another
example by iterating between generation, feedback, and revision without additional model training. Chain-of-Verification
(Dhuliawala et al., 2024) drafts an answer, plans verification questions, answers them independently, and then revises the
original answer. This procedure targets factual errors; dedicated critic agents are a possible multi-agent extension that requires
evaluation.
    These techniques seek reliability through different mechanisms. Self-consistency and debate broaden the set of candidate
reasoning paths; Chain-of-Agents distributes evidence processing across long inputs; reflection and critique revisit a provisional
answer to identify and repair errors (Wang et al., 2023b; Du et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Shinn et al., 2023; Madaan et al.,
2023; Dhuliawala et al., 2024). They are not interchangeable: sampling more paths helps only when those paths provide
meaningful diversity, sequential evidence transfer can lose information, and a critic may share the generator’s blind spots.
Combining mechanisms is possible, but each extra pass adds inference cost and requires an aggregation or stopping rule. The
appropriate choice therefore depends on the dominant failure mode: ambiguity among plausible answers, limited capacity to
process long evidence, or the need to audit a provisional response. Reliability should be evaluated against these failure modes
rather than inferred from the number of agents or reasoning rounds.

5. Development and evaluation resources for multi-agent collaborative QA
    The preceding sections examined how multi-agent collaborative QA systems are organized, implemented, selected, and
applied. This section reviews the resources needed to construct and assess such systems, following the four layers shown in
Fig. 5: foundation components, multi-agent orchestration frameworks, application-oriented resources, and benchmarks and
evaluation tools. The layers serve complementary functions: foundation components connect models and retrieval resources;
orchestration frameworks define agent roles and workflow control; application resources instantiate task-oriented capabilities;
and benchmarks and tools assess answer quality and collaboration. The figure is a conceptual map, not a requirement that
every system use a single tool from each layer.

5.1. Foundation layer: model and retrieval components
    The foundation layer supplies the interfaces and reusable components on which a QA workflow can be built. As illustrated
in Fig. 5, development frameworks such as LangChain and LlamaIndex provide representative model-interface and retrieval-
component resources. At this layer, the main concerns are connecting model services, preparing and retrieving evidence,
and making external data accessible to the system. These components support retrieval-grounded answering, but do not by
themselves assign responsibilities to multiple agents or determine how their work is coordinated.
    Interoperability protocols address part of this integration problem. For example, the Model Context Protocol (MCP)
standardizes connections between models or agents and external tools and data sources (Anthropic, 2024). Such interfaces can
support several orchestration frameworks, but do not specify role allocation, task decomposition, or decision authority. These
coordination functions belong to the orchestration layer discussed next.

5.2. Orchestration layer: multi-agent frameworks
     The orchestration layer determines how agents are defined, assigned work, and coordinated during execution. Figure 5
illustrates this layer with AutoGen, CrewAI, and MetaGPT (Wu et al., 2024; Moura, 2023; Hong et al., 2024). Across the
wider set of resources, some frameworks encode collaboration through predefined roles and procedures: for example, CAMEL
uses role-conditioned dialogue, while ChatDev and MetaGPT organize work through staged, role-based processes (Li et al.,
2023; Qian et al., 2024; Hong et al., 2024). Other frameworks expose more control flow to developers: AutoGen supports
configurable conversations, LangGraph represents workflows as state graphs, and AgentVerse provides a phased collaboration
process (Wu et al., 2024; Duan and Wang, 2024; Chen et al., 2024b). GPTSwarm explores optimization of agent connections.
DSPy, by contrast, focuses on metric-driven optimization of language-model pipelines and is therefore an adjacent toolkit, not
a like-for-like multi-agent framework (Zhuge et al., 2024; Khattab et al., 2024).
